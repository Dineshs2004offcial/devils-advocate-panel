# Case Study: A Failed SaaS Startup — College Conductor

## The Vision
College Conductor was a SaaS tool built for independent educational consultants (IECs) — professionals who help students find the right college or university. The founder's wife was herself an independent education consultant and had lamented the poor quality of the online tools available to run her business; she wanted a tool that could track the students she worked with. This is a classic "founder builds for a spouse/close contact's underserved niche" origin story, distinct from the more commonly cited "founder scratches their own itch" pattern.

## The Outcome
After three years of operation, the founder shut College Conductor down. The founder wrote a detailed public post-mortem explaining the reasons for the shutdown, describing it explicitly as "a chance to reflect" and to help other founders see potential pitfalls before they build a SaaS product on a similarly narrow niche.

## Relevance as a Comparison Case
College Conductor is a useful contrast case against the Apollo.io and Zenfetch case studies in this corpus because it represents a genuine "narrow vertical SaaS, insufficient market depth" failure mode, distinct from Apollo's unit-economics failure or Zenfetch's founder-market-fit failure — the independent educational consultant market may simply have been too small and/or too underfunded (IECs are often solo practitioners or small firms) to sustain a dedicated SaaS vendor at scale, regardless of product quality or execution.

## Broader Pattern Context
This failure sits within a well-documented broader pattern: "no market need" is the most commonly cited reason for startup failure, cited in an estimated 42% of startup post-mortems analyzed by CB Insights. Founders of failed SaaS startups frequently note in retrospect that early positive signals from a narrow set of engaged early adopters (in this case, presumably a handful of IECs who provided validating feedback) do not reliably predict whether a broader addressable market exists at a scale sufficient to build a venture-scale (or even sustainable lifestyle-scale) business.

## Lesson for Market-Sizing in a Fundraising Deck
This case underscores why VC due-diligence frameworks (see the VC section of this corpus) place heavy emphasis on market-size validation early, independent of product quality or founder passion for the problem — a well-built product for a real but small niche can still fail to reach sustainable scale, which is precisely the market-sizing risk institutional investors screen for before committing capital.
